Week 19: Complete final quality assurance and prepare the table for inspection

Final presentation should reveal the workmanship accurately, not disguise faults or add unapproved temporary fixes.

Theory 1

Final presentation and systematic inspection

Final presentation prepares the completed bedside table for teacher inspection without disguising faults or changing the approved design. Clean the table safely using the method directed in the workshop, removing dust, loose debris and handling marks without damaging the finish. Inspect the thick top for alignment and secure fitting, check that the tapered legs are consistent and stable, and confirm that the curved front apron matches the approved shape.

Examine joints for neat fit and movement, review the finish under good light, and ensure all exposed edges are safe to handle. Test storage components or hardware only where they appear in the approved design. Place the table on a stable, level surface so its proportions, workmanship and stability can be inspected clearly.

Prepare concise evidence labels that identify important features, checks and outcomes without repeating the full folio. Good presentation highlights the work accurately; it does not use props, camera angles or temporary adjustments to hide defects that should be recorded honestly.

Follow a fixed inspection route

Begin with safety and stability, then move through frame, joints, top, approved storage, surface and finish. A fixed route reduces the chance of overlooking a leg, underside or hidden fixing.

Present honestly

Use a clean, level area and suitable light. Do not hide a rocking leg with a temporary pad, close a sticky drawer for the photograph or use an angle that conceals an uneven front.

1
Priority order: Safety and function come before cosmetic extras. A stable, honest project is stronger than a decorative presentation that hides a fault.

Theory 2

Correct the cause, not only the symptom

Late faults often tempt rushed sanding, tightening or touching up. The final correction should be the least invasive action that addresses the actual cause without creating a new problem.

Confirm the reference. Check the table on a known level surface, use the approved drawing and inspect the relevant component separately where possible. A rocking table on an uneven floor does not prove unequal legs.

Protect completed work. A correction near finished surfaces, joints or hardware can cause more damage than the original fault. Mask or support the area as directed and stop if the repair requires changing the approved shape or structure.

Retest after correction. Repeat the same test that revealed the issue. Record whether the result is resolved, improved or still limited. Do not claim success because the adjustment is less visible.

Check

Repeat the original test on a reliable reference.

→
Correct

Use the smallest approved action that treats the cause.

→
Verify

Retest function, safety and appearance.

!
Late-stage restraint: Do not plane a leg, alter a joint or add an unapproved fixing simply because submission is close. Seek teacher advice.

Theory 3

Care, maintenance and safe handling

A well-made bedside table needs suitable care to retain its stability, finish and service life. Cleaning, routine maintenance and repair are different responsibilities.

Use the table as designed. Place it on a stable floor, avoid loads or impacts outside normal bedside use and keep heat, moisture and chemicals away from the finished surface. Lift the table by supporting the frame rather than dragging it by the top or an approved storage component.

Clean compatibly. Use only the teacher- or manufacturer-approved care method for the finish. Excess water, harsh cleaners or abrasive pads can damage the coating and timber.

Respond to faults early. Stop using the table if a leg, joint, top fixing or hardware becomes loose or unsafe. Routine checking may identify the problem, but structural repair should be completed by a suitable person using compatible materials and methods.

Cleaning

Remove normal dust or marks using a method compatible with the finish.

Maintenance

Inspect fixings, operation and surface condition before a small fault grows.

Repair

Correct damaged timber, joints or hardware with suitable knowledge and materials.

Handling

Lift and transport by supporting the frame; protect the top and tapered legs.

3
Key idea: Durability is part of sustainability. Correct use, maintenance and repair can extend the service life of the whole project.

Week 20: Submit, reflect, maintain and hand over responsibly

A professional finish includes organised evidence, safe care instructions and a complete workshop reset.

Theory 4

Final submission as quality assurance

Final submission should present a complete and organised record of the guided-course evidence that supports the finished bedside table. Check that the student name and class are correct on all submitted work. Confirm that each required module PDF is complete, readable and saved with a clear file name.

Where required, include a folio backup or export so the evidence is not lost or left only on one device. Select clear final photographs that show the completed slim tapered-leg table, including important details such as the top, legs, curved front apron, joints, finish and any approved storage feature. Make sure all digital evidence matches the approved project and reflects the work actually completed in the workshop.

Remove duplicate files, temporary versions and incomplete drafts before uploading or handing in the final submission. The website evidence should explain decisions, processes, checks and results, but it does not replace the practical project. The completed table remains the main evidence of workmanship, accuracy and safe workshop practice.

!
Submission integrity: Evidence must represent the student’s own project, decisions and checks. A generic or borrowed image breaks the evidence chain.

Theory 5

Reflection and transferable improvement

Reflection identifies what happened, why it happened and what should change next time. It is most useful when it names a specific practice rather than offering general praise or regret.

Use project evidence. Consider the accuracy of datums, matching tapers, joint fit, glue-up control, top fitting, storage clearances, surface preparation and finishing. Select one area that improved and explain what practice made the difference.

Identify a realistic next change. “Take more care” is too vague. A stronger improvement might be to compare all leg tapers before refinement, rehearse clamp placement before adhesive, or photograph the raking-light inspection before coating.

Transfer the learning. Explain how the improved method would support safety, accuracy, efficiency or quality on another timber project. Reflection should make future action clearer.

Reflection pattern

Evidence: what occurred and how you know.

Cause: the process or decision that influenced the result.

Next action: one specific method you would repeat or change.

Transfer: why that action matters on a future project.

?
Useful honesty: A limitation can show strong learning when the cause and future action are explained clearly.

Theory 6

Workshop reset and responsible handover

Reflection should consider the skills, decisions, safety practices and quality achieved across the full bedside table project. Use evidence from the completed slim tapered-leg table to identify what improved during construction and what still needs development. Consider the accuracy of the tapered legs, top alignment, curved front apron, joint fit, surface preparation and finish where these were part of the approved design.

Record one specific learning point, such as a marking, shaping, assembly or finishing technique that became more reliable through practice. Then identify one realistic improvement for a future project and explain how it would lead to safer, more accurate or more efficient work. Reflection is followed by a responsible workshop reset.

Return tools and equipment to their correct locations, clean benches and work areas, and sort reusable offcuts and waste according to workshop direction. Leave floors, machines and shared spaces clear and safe so the next group can begin work without avoidable hazards or delays.

Return the shared workshop to a known standard. Clean and return tools, remove offcuts and waste as directed, clear machines and benches, and leave floors and access routes safe. Report damaged, blunt or missing equipment rather than placing it back silently.

Protect project and records. Store or transport the table so the top, finish, tapered legs and hardware cannot be damaged. Confirm that digital evidence has been downloaded and that no personal work remains only in temporary browser storage.
